# The Jev bot layer (RL model + a TypeSafe System One second opinion)

A "Jev-<model>" bot (for example `Jev-RL-5`) is the trained ONNX model it names, playing exactly as the
plain `RL-5` bot does, with one addition: before each decision is taken, Jev (TypeSafe's System One model)
is asked the same question over the same legal options, and may change the answer.

Every decision a bot makes is covered:

| decision                | question id            | primitive | asked by default                              |
| ----------------------- | ---------------------- | --------- | --------------------------------------------- |
| which rondel space      | `rondel_space`         | Choice    | when the move looks questionable (see below)   |
| which bond to buy       | `bond_to_buy`          | Choice    | yes, every investor turn                       |
| which city builds       | `factory_city`         | Choice    | yes, every factory built                       |
| which unit to import    | `unit_to_buy`          | Choice    | yes, every unit bought                         |
| fight or retreat        | `fight_or_retreat`     | Noul      | yes, every battle offered to it                |
| pull a factory down     | `destroy_the_factory`  | Noul      | yes, every chance to                           |
| where a unit moves      | `where_this_unit_goes` | Choice    | no - level, and four times the cost            |
| upright or lying down   | `stand_upright`        | Noul      | no - same                                      |

`TypeSafe:Decisions` overrides the set: a comma-separated list of question ids, or `all` to switch the
last two back on. Both are implemented and tested; they are off because they cost four times as much for
no measured gain, not because they are missing or harmful.

The options always come from the shared bot path, never from the layer, so a layered bot never sees a move
the others cannot make (`.agents/AGENTS.md`, "No Bot-Identity Cheating"). An answer naming something that
was not offered is dropped, and so is one the model is surer of than Jev - a Choice below the confidence
gate, or a Noul too near a coin flip, where 0.5 means the two answers are equally likely rather than a
weak yes.

Decisions are also only asked about where they decide something: a rondel move the code can already see
is questionable, a unit whose destinations differ in what they would do, a posture that offers a battle
or shuts a factory down. That is about which calls are worth spending, never about which moves are
allowed - every legal option stays on the table either way.

## Where the pieces are

- `Server/Services/TypeSafe/TypeSafeClient.cs` - the endpoint (`POST https://api.typesafe.ai/v1/systemone`),
  Choice and Noul, one question per call, 8s timeout. Every failure returns null; nothing throws. Each
  answer is logged with what it cost in tokens, which is the only record of the spend on our side.
- `Server/Services/Bots/Strategies/JevRlBotStrategy.cs` - the rondel question.
- `Server/Services/Bots/Strategies/JevRlBotStrategy.Decisions.cs` - every other decision.
- `Server/Services/Bots/IBotStrategy.cs` - the `Reconsider...Async` hooks, one per decision, on the shared
  contract (the heuristics answer null to all of them). `RondelCandidate` is the legal-space record.
- `Server/Services/BotService.cs` - makes its own decision, then offers it to the strategy with the legal
  options beside it, and refuses an answer that is not among them. One log line per decision changed.
- `Server/Helpers/RondelOutlookHelper.cs` - what a rondel space would be worth from here, and whether a
  move looks questionable. Shared with the training reward, which is where two of the predicates began.
- Tests: `Tests/JevRondelLayerTests.cs` and `Tests/JevDecisionLayerTests.cs` (stubbed transport, no
  network), `Tests/JevLiveCallTests.cs` (one real call, skipped without a key).

## The API key

Read from configuration as `TypeSafe:ApiKey` - the same arrangement as `Jwt__Key`, never a checked-in file.

```bash
# development
dotnet user-secrets set "TypeSafe:ApiKey" "<key>" --project Server/Imperial2030.Server.csproj

# production (Azure App Service application setting, or the VPS service environment)
TypeSafe__ApiKey=<key>
```

Without a key the `Jev-...` bot types are not offered at all (`BotTypeCatalog`), and a game that already
has one plays the plain model. They are also left out of the random bot type a lobby assigns when no type
is asked for, so a casual "add bot" never spends quota.

## What Jev is told, and what it is not

The question carries the position as any player at the table sees it: the acting nation's power, treasury,
flags, factories, units and occupied home provinces; the government's cash, score and bonds; every nation's
power and marker; the other players' scores and bond interest. Each option carries what that action would
actually produce now - the taxation preview (`TaxationHelper.PreviewTaxation`), the investor preview
(`InvestorHelper.PreviewInterestPayment`), the production estimate, and the move's cost in millions.

Each option also states what it would cost to keep what it produces: Production says how many units the
nation may still have at all (China has 10 armies and 6 fleets, no more) and that every unit costs the
treasury 1m of soldiers' pay at every Taxation and does nothing until a Maneuver moves it; Maneuver says
how many regions next to its units could actually take a flag - not already its own, holding no foreign
unit, and not a home province, which never takes one - and what a flag is worth in tax revenue. Without
those two numbers the layer read Production as free units and moved a China that already had nine.

It is never told who or what plays the other nations: no bot types, strategy names or model names
(`.agents/AGENTS.md`, "No Bot-Identity Cheating"). `NothingAboutHowTheOpponentsDecideIsSent` asserts it.

A space whose identical twin is legal and cheaper is not offered: Production and Maneuver each sit on two
spaces (p.6), and the dearer one buys the same action for more money. The question says when the model's
own pick was dropped for that reason.

## Guarantees

- Inference only. Training decides through the training server's action mask and never calls out
  (`TrainingIsNeverAsked`), so a training run cannot stall or drift because of an outside service.
- The ONNX state vector and action space are untouched, so every existing model keeps working as it did.
- No key, a timeout, a 429, a malformed answer, or an answer naming a space that is not legal: the model's
  own move stands. The layered bot is never worse off than the model it wraps for want of an answer.
- An answer is only taken when its confidence is at least `JevRlBotStrategy.MinimumConfidence` (0.5). That
  is a starting point, not a measured optimum - see below.

## What the measurements say

Two harnesses, both skipped unless `TypeSafe__ApiKey` is in the environment:

- `Tests/JevHeadToHeadTests.cs` - the layered bot and the plain model at the same table, four heuristics
  in the other seats, each deal played twice with the two seats swapped. Reports wins, average score, the
  paired per-game difference and its 95% interval.
  `$env:JEV_H2H_PAIRS = "50"` for 100 games; `JEV_GATE` sets the confidence gate and `JEV_ASK_ALWAYS=1`
  turns off the questionable-move filter, so any configuration can be measured against the model it wraps.
- `Tests/JevDecisionQualityTests.cs` - shadow play: the bot moves where the model says, Jev is asked the
  same question anyway, and every answer is scored against the position with the training server's own
  predicates. One run of calls can then be re-examined at any gate without paying for the answers again.
  `JEV_CALL_BUDGET`, `JEV_GAMES`, `JEV_ASK_EVERY` (ask one decision in N, so the budget spreads over whole
  games rather than openings).

### What it comes to

**The advice is real, and RL-5 does not need it.** Layered over the plain heuristic bot it is worth about
14 points a game, measured on the same games played twice - the one interval in this file that excludes
zero. Layered over RL-5 it is worth nothing measurable, across several hundred paired games and every
subset of decisions.

That is not a verdict on Jev. It is what the model is: over 24 games against the heuristics RL-5 finished
first in 21 by an average of 46 points and made **no** flagged bad move in 478 rondel decisions - nothing
wasted, no taxation worth 5 power walked past, never the dearer of two identical spaces. There is no room
above a bot that is not making mistakes.

### Same game, twice (Tests/JevPairedRerunTests.cs)

The sharp instrument. `BotRandom` seeds every bot decision, so a game can be replayed exactly: once as
the bot plays it, once with the layer on. The two runs are identical until the first decision the layer
changes, so the difference is the layer rather than the deal. `TheSameSeedPlaysTheSameGame` guards it and
needs no key.

It cuts the per-game spread from 42-48 points to 12-22, which is worth roughly ten times the games:

| base bot | opponents    | decisions   | pairs | ahead | behind | identical | difference        |
| -------- | ------------ | ----------- | ----- | ----- | ------ | --------- | ----------------- |
| Default  | heuristics   | all         |    15 |    10 |      4 |         1 | **+13.93 +/- 11.35** |
| RL-5     | heuristics   | all         |    15 |     5 |      6 |         4 | -8.07 +/- 13.33   |
| RL-5     | RL-5, RL-4.. | all         |    25 |     8 |      4 |        13 | +1.76 +/- 6.45    |
| RL-5     | RL-5, RL-4.. | rondel only |    60 |    14 |     10 |        36 | +0.93 +/- 4.28    |
| RL-5     | RL-5, RL-4.. | moves only  |    25 |     4 |      2 |        19 | +1.20 +/- 4.66    |
| RL-5     | RL-5, RL-4.. | bonds only  |    25 |     0 |      0 |        25 | exactly 0         |

Bonds deserve their own line: over 257 bond questions on the hard table Jev picked the bot's own choice
**every single time**. Not a close call it lost - no disagreement at all. Whatever a bond is worth, the
two of them read it the same way.

### The table has to be hard

RL-5 beats the heuristics 21 games in 24. Nothing can be shown to improve a bot in games it has already
won by 46 points, which is what the first round of measurement was doing. Against the other RL
generations it finishes first in 7 of 24 and loses by 27 on average - games close enough for a decision
to matter. `JEV_OPPONENTS` sets the table; use the RL generations for anything that is meant to prove
something.

### Where the model's own bad moves are (Tests/RlBlunderMiningTests.cs)

Plays seeded games, records every rondel decision with the position it was made in, and reports the moves
from the games the model lost worst. No API calls. Against the heuristics: 0 flagged moves in 478. On the
hard table: 1 in 310 (Europe at 0 power walking past a Taxation worth 8). The predicates that caught 25
mistakes in 62 chances on the training server barely fire in these games, so a second opinion aimed at
blunders has almost nothing to aim at here - the live games that started this work are a different
population, and the user's own screenshots are still the only sighting of the Investor loop.

### Earlier results, kept for the lesson

| configuration                              | games | ahead | behind | avg score difference | calls per game |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----- | ----- | ------ | -------------------- | -------------- |
| rondel only, questionable moves            |   270 |   139 |    126 | -2.0 +/- 5.3         | ~2             |
| every decision, with the board             |   130 |    58 |     70 | -1.8 +/- 7.4         | ~31-36         |
| the default set, with the board            |    90 |    51 |     38 | +5.9 +/- 8.9         | ~9             |
| every decision, starved question (the bug) |    52 |    19 |     32 | -13.4 +/- 12.2       | ~31-43         |

The last row measured a question with no map in it: the per-unit questions listed region names and
nothing else, while the policy they were compared against reads the whole board. Fixed, and the collapse
does not reproduce. The lesson is the one that cost the most here - before concluding a model is bad at
something, read the question it was asked.

A bug the measurement found, not the tests: a decision taken before the nation has a marker on the rondel
- an investor turn in the first round - threw inside the bot turn and stopped the game.
`ADecisionBeforeTheMarkerIsOnTheRondelStillWorks` covers it now.

### Shadow-play diagnostics (105 answers over 14 complete games)

- Jev agreed with the model's move 67% of the time. When it agreed its median confidence was 0.80; when it
  disagreed, 0.50 - it is least sure exactly where it differs.
- Its disagreements are not random: Production -> Maneuver (11), Investor -> Maneuver (7),
  Production -> Taxation (5). It prefers acting on the board to building more of what is already there.
- The known-mistake predicates fired on 1 of 105 of the model's own moves in bot-vs-bot play. They were
  measured on the training server, where RL-5 walked past a 5+ power taxation on 25 of 62 chances; against
  these opponents those chances barely arise. The predicates are not a scoring rubric for self-play.

## What is still worth trying

1. **More games.** At ~2 calls a game the harness is cheap: 120 games is 2.6 minutes and ~220 calls. A
   real 5-point effect needs roughly 300 games to separate from zero, which is one afternoon of runs -
   worth doing before believing any change to the question helped.
2. **The other decisions.** The rondel is one choice a turn. Bond buying, the factory city and maneuver
   destinations all go through the same bot contract and none of them have a second opinion. The live
   complaints that started this work were about investment moves as much as rondel moves.
3. **The question itself.** Each fact added so far changed behaviour measurably (soldiers' pay and unit
   caps moved it off Production; the power-factor arithmetic raised its confidence enough to double the
   override rate). Facts it still does not get: what the opponents threaten, and what a flag is worth
   against the taxation chart's steps rather than 1m flat.
